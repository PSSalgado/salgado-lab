It was October 19, 2001. In the wake of the 9/11 attacks in New York, safety measures in airports were strict: only passengers with a valid boarding pass were allowed inside the terminal at Porto's airport, in Portugal. And so I had to say goodbye to my parents and my boyfriend just outside the doors, then walk on my own, my things packed in 2 big suitcases and a bag, a backpack with a computer on my back. I don't really remember if it was raining or not, just the plane full of Celtic fans singing and a feeling of exhilaration and dread, of sadness and joy, all mixed up.

I was on my way to do a PhD in structural biology, at Oxford, UK. Three, maybe four years and then I would be back home to Porto, I thought. When I got to Oxford, my supervisor, [Jon Grimes](https://www.strubi.ox.ac.uk/research/professor-jonathan-m-grimes), was waiting at the bus stop and helped me carry my bags to the hall where I would stay for now. It was all new and exciting, and scary. Over the next couple of days, I also met with my co-supervisor [Dave Stuart](https://www.strubi.ox.ac.uk/research/professor-david-stuart-frs), [Dennis Bamford](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/jvi.00594-26) and his team, our collaborators from Finland, and everyone in [Strubi](https://www.strubi.ox.ac.uk/), who would become friends over the next few years.

Those three to four years turned into 25. I didn't exactly plan it that way, it just so happens that the UK has been the place offering the opportunities I was looking for at each step of my research career.

First, a postdoc once I completed my PhD. London was calling, it had been calling since 1995, when I visited with my parents.

For a while, I thought I didn't want to be a PI, I liked being on the bench, doing experiments, collecting data so a second postdoc, now at Imperial College London, was the next choice. But the more experienced I became, the more I realised I liked being the one asking the questions, designing the experiments and I changed my mind.

I started planning for fellowship and job applications, a route to independence. The path there was somewhat unexpected, with a detour as a postdoc in [Neil Fairweather](https://profiles.imperial.ac.uk/n.fairweather)'s lab, defining *C. difficile* as my "niche" topic and a lectureship at Newcastle University providing a home to set up the "Salgado lab".

Moving to the North East of England meant being close to the sea again, something I missed so much!

*(Did you know Oxford is among the cities where you need to drive longest to get to see the sea?! I didn't when I moved there and it was one of the things I missed the most in those first weeks and months after moving there!)*

And the North Sea is so beautiful, the beaches are amazing! Shame it's too cold for most of the year to enjoy a swim, but I love being so close I can just hop on the car and sit there, watching the waves crash and letting that familiar salted smell fill my lungs. It takes me home - or is this home now?...

When I first asked myself that question, I remember thinking that I'm really lucky to have two places I call home: Portugal, where I grew up, where my family and friends are; and the UK, where I've built my career, my adult life.

This happy feeling of multiple roots was shattered in June 2016, when suddenly a feeling of "otherness" was overwhelming. Not surprising, but shocking nevertheless, Brexit really made me question if there was a future for us here. Despite the personal impact, my career was going well, and the UK still was a good place to do science, even if a bit less so and certainly feeling less open and welcoming. So, we stayed and deepened our roots here, built a family as well as a career, and became British citizens.

Things have changed dramatically since that October day, when I got on a plane, ready for the challenges, both in science and in life. Research funding, especially for discovery science, is now so competitive in certain calls that a lottery would probably be better... The university system is struggling with multiple challenges and demands. Immigration remains a hot topic. Times are uncertain but I'm still here.

25 years, 1 PhD, >30 papers, 5 grants, 6 supervised PhDs, 8 house moves, 2 cats, 1 son and many friends later, for me, the UK is still the place to do research. It is home too.
